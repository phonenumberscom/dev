Making our inventory visible on Google — the plain-English plan

For the whole team. Based on studying how atom.com (the domain marketplace) structures their site for Google — verified from their actual sitemap files, 2026-08-02.

The idea in one paragraph

Every product should have its own web page that Google can read, and Google should be handed a list of all those pages. Atom does this with domains: every domain they sell has a page like atom.com/name/OakSnap with a unique paragraph about that name, and they give Google list files containing roughly 900,000 of these pages. When someone searches for something specific, one of those pages catches the search. Our numbers already have their own URLs (/en-us/numbers/1719LAWYERS) — but the pages are blank to Google and we've never handed Google the list. This plan fixes that.

What we learned from Atom's setup (with the takeaway for us)

1 · One page per product, with unique text

Each Atom listing page has a short, specific write-up ("Vialta is a six-letter symphony…"). Not a template sentence with the name swapped in — actual specific detail. That uniqueness is why Google bothers to keep the pages.

For us: every number page gets 2–3 sentences generated from what we already know about it: what it spells, the area code's city, the pattern type, the memorability grade, which industry it suits. "(719) LAWYERS is a toll-free vanity number that spells LAWYERS — a memorable line for a law practice, ready to activate today." We have better data per item than Atom does.

2 · The "list files" (sitemaps) are organized by page type, in chunks

Atom doesn't have one giant list. Their robots.txt points to a master index that splits into families: marketing pages (159 URLs), premium listings (9 files × 50,000), standard listings (8 × 50,000), category pages (1,332), registrar pages (514), blog. Files max out at 50,000 URLs each — that's the sitemap standard.

For us: keep the current sitemap for site pages, and add new families: sitemap-numbers-premium.xml (vanity + premium tiers first), later sitemap-numbers-local.xml, chunked at 50K. Add the index to robots.txt. Our 461 area-code pages already follow this pattern — extend it, don't invent it.

3 · They keep the bulk inventory OFF the main site

Interesting trick: Atom's premium listings live on atom.com/name/…, but their huge standard-tier catalog lives on a separate subdomain (domains.atom.com/lpd/name/…). That keeps hundreds of thousands of thinner pages from diluting the main site's reputation.

For us: same principle, simpler version — index the premium/vanity tier first (pages with genuinely unique stories), and hold the mass of ordinary local numbers back until tier one proves out. Quality of what Google samples matters more than quantity submitted.

4 · They run a second engine: category pages

Atom has 1,332 pages like /business-name-generator/coffee, /business-name-generator/insurance — one landing page per niche, each a curated collection. This catches "coffee shop name ideas"-type searches that no single product page can.

For us: we already have the area-code family (461 pages ✓). The equivalents we're missing: per-industry pages (law, medical, real estate — "phone numbers for law firms"), per-pattern pages (repeating digits, sequential, mirror), and eventually per-city. Each is a filtered search view given a real URL, title, and intro paragraph.

5 · They explicitly welcome AI crawlers

The first rule in Atom's robots.txt is Allow: GPTBot. AI assistants that recommend products are a traffic source they're courting deliberately.

For us: check our robots.txt doesn't block GPTBot/ClaudeBot/PerplexityBot. Given our AI Overview win this week, we want every AI crawler reading the catalog.

The canonical shield — keep it, lift it in step

Today every listing page's canonical points to the search page — deliberately. That was the right protection while listings were JavaScript shells: it stopped Google from crawling thousands of blank near-identical pages (the CountryCode lesson). The migration rule: a page keeps the shield until it has real server-rendered content — then, in the same deploy, its canonical flips to self-referencing and its URL joins the sitemap. Never lift the shield before content ships; never ship content still wearing the shield.

The one mistake to avoid (we've been burned once)

No duplicate pages across locales. On country codes we ended up with the same page under /en-us/… and /en-ca/… and Google had to guess. For numbers: a number belongs to one country — US numbers exist ONLY under /en-us/numbers/…, Canadian ONLY under /en-ca/numbers/…. The other prefix 301-redirects. Every page carries a self-referencing canonical tag (once the shield lifts). Sitemaps list canonical URLs only. Build this in from day one.
